The Enneagram, without the wishful thinking

The enneagram is a model of nine personality patterns, each organized around a core fear and core desire. It describes why you do what you do — the motivation under the behavior — which is also exactly why people mistype: two people can act identically for completely different reasons, and a psychological condition can imitate a type's behavior without sharing its motivation.

This site covers all nine types with their wings and growth/stress arrows, catalogs the most common mislabelings (like ADHD wearing a Type 7 costume), and offers two tests — a quick one and an exhaustive one — that screen for those look-alikes instead of ignoring them.

The three centers of intelligence

Body / Gut · 8, 9, 1

Instinct-led types whose core emotional issue is anger: Eights express it, Nines forget it, Ones repress and moralize it. Their concern is autonomy and control.

Heart / Feeling · 2, 3, 4

Image-aware types whose core emotional issue is shame: Twos earn worth through giving, Threes through achieving, Fours through being unique. Their concern is identity and value.

Head / Thinking · 5, 6, 7

Strategy-led types whose core emotional issue is fear: Fives retreat from it, Sixes scan for it, Sevens outrun it. Their concern is security and guidance.

Wings

Your wing is one of the two types adjacent to yours on the circle, flavoring your core type without replacing it. A One with a Nine wing (1w9) is cooler and more detached; a One with a Two wing (1w2) is warmer and more people-focused. Most people lean toward one wing, some draw on both, and the lean can shift across life. Every type page here describes both wings — see the 9 types.

Arrows: growth and stress

Each type is connected by the symbol's inner lines to two other types. In security and growth you pick up the healthy qualities of one (the growth or integration arrow); under sustained stress you slide toward the average-to-unhealthy qualities of the other (the stress or disintegration arrow). Example: a Seven grows toward the focused depth of Five and stresses toward the critical rigidity of One. Arrows explain why you can "act like" a type you're not — one more reason typing by behavior alone fails.

Why mistyping is the norm, not the exception

The core problem

Tests and observers see behavior; the enneagram is defined by motivation. Restlessness might be Seven avoidance-of-pain — or ADHD. Constant worry might be Six vigilance — or an anxiety disorder. Melancholy might be Four depth — or a depressive episode. This site flags those look-alikes explicitly on the mislabelings page, and both tests include screening items so your result comes with warnings where warranted.
