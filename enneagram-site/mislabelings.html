Common Mislabelings

The enneagram types are defined by motivation; everything anyone can observe — including your own introspection on a test — is behavior. Two very different engines can drive the same behavior, and the two big failure modes are:

  1. A condition mistaken for a type — neurology, mood, or trauma imitating an enneagram pattern (ADHD reading as Type 7 is the classic).
  2. A type mistaken for another type — surface behavior shared by two types with different motivations (a counterphobic Six reading as an Eight).

1 · Conditions that imitate types

Rule of thumb: type patterns are lifelong, context-general strategies you broadly endorse. Clinical patterns tend to have an onset, feel like an affliction, fail even when you're motivated, and often come with bodily signatures (sleep, appetite, startle, sensory overload). ⚑ ADHD ≠ Type 7 (or 8)

The overlap: novelty-seeking, many unfinished projects, restlessness, impulsive decisions, hating boredom and constraint.

The tell: a Seven's scatter is motivated — the mind flees pain and limitation toward the next positive possibility, and a Seven can focus superbly on what delights them. ADHD attention fails even during loved activities, has been present since childhood in every context, and comes with executive slips (time-blindness, losing things) that no reframe fixes. ADHD impulsivity plus a hot temper also gets mislabeled Eight — but Eight intensity is about control and justice, not disinhibition.

⚑ Anxiety disorders ≠ Type 6

The overlap: scanning for danger, worst-case thinking, reassurance-seeking, trouble deciding.

The tell: Six vigilance is a coherent stance the Six mostly endorses — preparation feels useful. Clinical anxiety feels like an affliction: physical, uncontrollable, often worse than the situation warrants by the person's own judgment. An anxious person of any type will score Six-ish on tests while the anxiety is loud.

⚑ Depression ≠ Type 4 or 9

The overlap: melancholy, withdrawal, low energy, feeling different or checked-out.

The tell: Four melancholy is a familiar emotional home that coexists with vivid aliveness; Nine placidity is lifelong and mostly comfortable. A depressive episode descends — weeks of anhedonia with sleep, appetite, and energy changes that feel unlike one's usual self. Never type yourself in the middle of an episode.

⚑ Autism ≠ Type 5

The overlap: social withdrawal, deep expertise interests, discomfort with intrusion, preferring observation to participation.

The tell: Five withdrawal is a strategy for conserving scarce energy and feeling competent. Autistic traits are neurodevelopmental: lifelong differences in reading unwritten social rules, sensory sensitivity, and interests pursued for their intrinsic pull. Many autistic people are not Fives at all — the withdrawal just photographs the same.

⚑ Trauma ≠ Type 6, 8, or 9

The overlap: hypervigilance (reads as 6), armored control and distrust (reads as 8), numbing, fawning, and merging (reads as 9).

The tell: trauma responses have an origin story — a before and after — plus intrusions, startle, and avoidance of specific reminders. Type patterns feel native and general. Untangling type from trauma often genuinely requires professional support.

⚑ OCD / OCPD ≠ Type 1

The overlap: high standards, order, control, discomfort with error.

The tell: a One's standards are ideals they'd defend as right. OCD rituals are unwanted intrusions the person often knows are irrational; OCPD rigidity persists even when the person agrees it's wrecking their efficiency and relationships.

How to type yourself honestly

  • Type the engine, not the paint. For any trait, ask "what am I afraid would happen if I stopped?" The answer points at the core fear.
  • Look at your whole life, not the last stressful year. Arrows and conditions both distort recent behavior.
  • If a screening flag fires on our tests, treat your type result as provisional and consider a professional evaluation for the flagged pattern.
  • Let the type explain you, not excuse you. If your "type" gives you a pass on something a clinician could help with, the label is doing harm.
