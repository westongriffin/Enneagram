Take a Test

Both tests ask you to rate statements from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Answer for how you've been across your whole adult life, not just lately — and answer for who you are, not who you'd like to be. Alongside the type items, both tests include screening items for patterns that famously imitate enneagram types (ADHD, anxiety, depression, autism, trauma, OCD/OCPD, social anxiety). If your answers fit one of those look-alike patterns and it overlaps your top types, your result will carry a ⚑ flag explaining the possible mislabeling.

Quick test

A first hypothesis with a coarse look-alike check. Two formats: rate statements (24 items, agree–disagree) or multiple choice (14 scenario questions where you pick the answer that fits best — good if you tend to agree with everything).

The complete assessment: type, wing, instinct stacking and subtype, full look-alike screening, and built-in validity checks. Two formats: rate statements (100 items, including reverse-keyed and consistency items) or multiple choice (35 scenario, either/or, and instinct questions).

How these tests are built

Motivation-first items

Questions target the why under behavior (core fears and desires), because two types can share every behavior and differ only in motive — the thing most free enneagram tests never ask about.

Look-alike screening

Separate scales for ADHD, anxiety, depression, autism, trauma, OCD/OCPD, social anxiety, and hypomania flag when a clinical pattern may be inflating a type score — and when one fires, a sensitivity check re-scores your types without the contested questions, showing whether your result survives the overlap.

Response-bias defenses

The 100-item version includes reverse-keyed items (so habitual agreeing can't fake a type), social-desirability catches, and paired consistency questions that flag rushed answering. The multiple-choice format resists over-agreement entirely.

Honest limits

These are serious self-report instruments, but they are not normed or clinically validated, and no personality test diagnoses anything. Results are hypotheses — the gold standard is sustained self-observation against the type descriptions.

Before you start: this is a self-reflection tool. It cannot diagnose any condition, and its mislabeling flags are hypotheses, not findings. If you're currently in a depressive episode, an anxiety spike, or fresh trauma, your answers will reflect the state more than the trait — the honest move is to retake it when the storm has passed, and to bring the state itself to a professional.
